Much of the AI governance at community banks and credit unions is built
around a person. Who is allowed to use the tool. What they may paste
into it. Who reviews what comes out. That model made sense when AI meant an
employee typing into a chat window and reading the answer.

It does not fit the next thing, and the next thing is already shipping.

OpenAI now offers team tasks in ChatGPT Business and Enterprise. A team task
is recurring work — a weekly summary, a status post, a draft built from a
folder of documents — that runs in the cloud on a schedule or a trigger. The
part that matters for you is in OpenAI's own guidance: team tasks "run in the
cloud under the team's service account, using the task's saved instructions
and the connections configured for the team." Creating one, OpenAI adds,
"does not make it a task in your personal account."

In plain terms: the work no longer belongs to the employee who set it up. It
belongs to an identity that is not a person. If your institution allows
scheduled or connected AI work — or is about to — that identity needs the
same discipline you already apply to every other service account in your
environment.

## What actually changed

Three details in the guidance do most of the work.

**The data and the actions come from the connection, not the user.** Each
workspace connection, OpenAI says, "uses a designated account. That
account's permissions determine which data and actions are available." Team
members "can reuse approved access without each person connecting a personal
account." That is convenient. It also means that whoever can edit a task's
instructions can direct work through an account they may not personally
hold the rights to. The permission sits in the connection; the intent sits
in the instructions.

**Sharing a plugin is not the same as granting access.** "Adding or sharing a
plugin is separate from connecting an account," the guidance says. A plugin
supplies tools and instructions; the connection supplies the access. An
approval that covers one does not cover the other, and a vendor review that
looks only at the plugin has looked at half the picture.

**Stopping a task does not stop a run.** This is the line I would put in
front of every operations leader: "Don't rely on pausing or deleting a task
to interrupt an active run." OpenAI also tells users to "check the task's
run history before trying again, especially if the task sends messages or
changes files." In other words, a scheduled job can still be mid-action when
someone hits pause, and a retry can do the same thing twice.

None of this is a flaw in the product. It is the normal behavior of
automation. The risk is in treating it as a chat feature.

## Why "shared" makes the governance harder, not easier

The pitch for shared tasks is continuity. When the person who built the
Monday report is on vacation, the report still runs. Nobody's personal
account becomes a single point of failure.

That is a real benefit. But continuity cuts both ways. A task that keeps
running when its creator is out also keeps running when its creator leaves
the institution, changes roles, or simply stops paying attention to it. The
instructions persist. The connection persists. The schedule persists. If no
one owns the task by name, no one notices when its output starts to drift or
its source documents go stale.

A few other details in the guidance point the same way:

- Team tasks "don't automatically inherit the creator's personal saved
  memories, Custom Instructions, or chat history." A task that worked when
  its author tested it in their own chat may behave differently when it runs
  as the team.
- "A model available in your own account may be unavailable to the team's
  service account." The thing you tested and the thing that runs may not be
  the same.
- "An unattended run can't complete a new app sign-in for you." When a connection's credential lapses, the task can't repair it on its own. Someone has to notice and reconnect.
- "Access to a run doesn't automatically give you access to its linked
  source documents or files created in connected apps." The person reviewing
  the output may not be able to see what it was built from.

Each of these is manageable. Together they describe something that should be
inventoried, owned, and reviewed like any other non-human identity. An
employee's AI habit is a different kind of object.

## This is an access-review problem you already know how to solve

You do not need a new framework for this. You need to extend the one you
have.

On September 29, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman
opened the Community Bank Cyber Workshop by restating the basics examiners
expect. Her list included up-to-date asset inventories and "strong identity
and access controls." She was speaking about cyber risk broadly, and about
AI's role in it. She was not speaking about any product, and I am not
suggesting she addressed team tasks. But the control she named is the right
one. A service account that can read your documents and post to your
channels is an identity. It belongs in the inventory and the access review.

Most community institutions already review privileged human accounts on a
schedule. Fewer have written down every service account, every shared
connection, and every scheduled job that acts on their data. AI tasks are
about to make that list longer. The institutions that handle this well will
be the ones that add AI identities to the review they already run, rather
than inventing a separate AI process that nobody maintains.

## Before you approve a shared task: ten fields

For each proposed team task, require a one-page record. If a field can't be
filled in, the task isn't ready.

1. **Business owner.** A named person accountable for what the task produces.
   Not a team. Not "IT."
2. **Technical owner.** The person who can change, pause, or delete it — and
   who knows how to check a run in progress.
3. **Service account and team.** Which team owns the task, and therefore which
   service account it runs under.
4. **Connection and connected account.** Which connections are enabled for the
   team, and which designated account sits behind each one. That account's
   rights are the task's rights.
5. **Source scope.** The specific folders, sites, or systems the task reads.
   Narrower than "everything the connection can see."
6. **Destination and action authority.** Where output goes and what the task
   may do: draft, post, send, edit, create. Writing to a channel is a
   different decision from writing a draft.
7. **Approval points.** Which actions still require a person to confirm. The
   guidance notes that unattended runs do not remove approval requirements
   for actions in connected apps. Know which ones apply.
8. **Run evidence.** Who reviews run history, how often, and what they check
   against the source documents.
9. **Cost owner.** OpenAI says workspace administrators manage spending
   controls for team tasks, and that personal limits do not set the limits
   for shared work. Someone should own that number.
10. **Retry rule and emergency stop.** What happens after a failure, who
    checks for duplicate actions before a rerun, and how an active run is
    actually halted — since pausing the task is not enough.

Field 4 is the easiest to skip, and it matters most. The connected
account determines what the task can reach. If that account is a
broadly privileged administrator, so is the task.

## What I would do in the next 90 days

**This month: find what already exists.** Ask your ChatGPT workspace
administrator for a list of teams, team tasks, and enabled connections, along
with any personal scheduled tasks staff have set up. Use OpenAI's role-based
controls, where your plan has them, to limit who can create teams and team
tasks while you take stock. For each existing task, identify the service
account and the connected account behind it.

**Next month: apply least privilege and name owners.** For every task you
keep, fill in the ten fields. Replace broadly privileged connected accounts
with accounts scoped to what the task needs. Retire tasks no one will own.
Decide which actions — sending, posting, editing — require a person in the
loop, and confirm that setting in the connected app, not just in ChatGPT.

**By day 90: test and review.** Pick one task and test the boundaries:
pause it mid-run and check what completed; let a credential lapse and see
how the failure surfaces; rerun after a failure and look for duplicates.
Then add AI service accounts, shared connections, and scheduled tasks to
your next access review, alongside the human accounts already there. Review
the first month of run history for every task in production.

If you use a different platform, the same questions apply. Other vendors are
adding reusable instructions and scheduled work of their own. The vendor
names change. The governance object — a persistent
task with its own identity, data access, and authority — does not.

## The point

A shared AI task is the moment AI stops being something an employee uses and
becomes something that runs on your behalf. That deserves a decision, not a
toggle. Before your teams set up recurring AI work, make sure every task has
a named owner, an identity you've scoped, evidence someone reviews, and a way
to stop it that actually works.

A schedule only proves the task ran on time. It doesn't prove the task did
the right thing, or that it was allowed to.
