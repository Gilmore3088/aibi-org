The short answer is yes — under three conditions. The tool is one your
institution has approved. The work contains no customer, member, or
confidential institution information unless the tool is approved for it.
And a person reviews the output before it goes anywhere that matters.

The longer answer matters because your staff are not waiting for
permission. If the institution has not said anything, employees are
already deciding for themselves, on personal phones, under consumer
terms of service you have never read.

## The risk is the input, not the tool

No federal rule singles out ChatGPT or any other AI product. GAO's 2025
review found that regulators primarily oversee AI through the laws and guidance
that already exist. For a community bank or credit union, the ones that
matter here are familiar: your information security program, your
privacy obligations for customer and member information, and your
third-party risk management.

Seen that way, pasting a loan file into a free chatbot is not an "AI
problem." It is the same problem as emailing that file to a personal
Gmail account. You already have the rules. AI just makes the mistake
faster and more tempting.

## Draw the data line in three tiers

Staff follow rules they can apply in the moment. Three tiers is the most
an employee will hold in their head:

than any policy paragraph.

## What staff can safely do today

With an approved general-purpose tool and only public or internal
information, the useful work is real:

- Drafting and tightening routine emails, letters, and procedures
- Summarizing a public regulation or a long vendor document you are
  allowed to share
- Turning meeting notes with no customer details into an action list
- Creating training scenarios with invented customers
- Rewriting member-facing copy for plain language before compliance
  review

Every one of these produces a draft. A person reviews it, edits it, and
owns it.

## What never goes into a public or unapproved tool

Make this list short and absolute:

- Customer or member names, account or card numbers, SSNs, dates of birth
- Loan applications, credit reports, financial statements, appraisals
- Examination reports, findings, or correspondence with your regulator
- Board materials, strategy, merger discussions, unreleased financials
- Passwords, system configurations, or security details
- Suspicious Activity Report content, including whether a SAR exists,
  and BSA/AML investigation details — these stay out of every AI tool
  unless compliance approves an exception in writing

## Give people an approved option

The fastest way to end shadow use is to replace it. Approving one
enterprise-grade tool for internal drafting — after your vendor
management team reviews the contract terms on data retention, training
on your inputs, and access controls — gives staff a legitimate place to
work. Our [IT review packet](/security/it-approval) lists what to ask
for.

Then write it down. The data line and the approved tool belong in your
[AI acceptable use policy](/guides/ai-acceptable-use-policy-community-bank),
and every approved use belongs in your AI use case inventory. The
examination framework state bank supervisors released in September 2026
asks about both. Your state decides how much to use it, and federal
examiners may well ask the same questions.
