"A human reviews all AI output" is the most common sentence in bank AI
policies, and one of the least examined. Review that is not defined
becomes review that does not happen — or happens as a glance and a
click. The fix is to decide, for each use, who reviews, what they check,
and how you know the review is real.

## Match the review to the stakes

Four levels cover almost every use at a community institution:

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Level</th><th>Applies to</th><th>Review</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Self-check</td><td>Internal drafts, summaries, and notes with no customer or member data</td><td>The person who used the tool reads and edits before using it</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Qualified review</td><td>Customer- or member-facing communications, marketing copy, procedures</td><td>A second person with subject-matter authority approves before release</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Documented review</td><td>Anything placed in an official record: loan files, BSA/AML case notes, complaint responses</td><td>Reviewer verifies against source documents and the review is recorded</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Decision authority</td><td>Credit, eligibility, account actions</td><td>AI may inform; a qualified person makes and owns the decision</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Record the level for each entry in your
[AI use case inventory](/guides/ai-use-case-inventory-bank). It turns a
vague policy promise into something an auditor can test.

## What reviewers should check

Give every reviewer a short, specific checklist. For most bank uses:

- **Facts.** Every number, date, name, rate, and citation matches the
  source. AI tools can state invented facts fluently.
- **Completeness.** Nothing material was dropped from a summary.
- **Data.** No restricted information appears where it should not.
- **Tone and accuracy for the audience.** Nothing misleading, overpromising,
  or out of line with disclosures.
- **Compliance.** Required language is present; nothing creates a UDAAP or
  fair lending concern.

## Keep it from becoming a rubber stamp

Review fails quietly when volume outruns attention. Watch three signals:

1. **Change rate.** Track how often reviewers edit or reject AI output. A
   rate that drops to nothing over hundreds of items deserves a look.
2. **Sampling.** Have a second person periodically re-review a small
   sample of approved items. Findings tell you whether the checklist and
   the training work.
3. **Volume.** If one reviewer is approving more than they could
   reasonably read, the process is broken regardless of what the policy
   says.

## Credit decisions need the strongest line

Where AI touches lending, accountability has to sit with a person and the
reasons have to be real. Regulation B requires specific principal reasons
for adverse action, and reasons based only on failing to achieve a
qualifying score are insufficient.
[More on AI and adverse action notices](/guides/ai-adverse-action-notices-regulation-b).

## When less review is acceptable

Some narrow automation — routing, classification of internal documents,
formatting — can run without item-by-item review if it is tested,
monitored, and low impact. Make that a deliberate decision, write down the
monitoring, and revisit it when the tool or the use changes. "We stopped
checking because it was always right" is not a control.
