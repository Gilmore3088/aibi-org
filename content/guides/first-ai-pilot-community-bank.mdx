The most common first AI project at a community institution is the wrong
one: a customer-facing chatbot, chosen because it is visible and a vendor
offered a demo. It carries the most risk, is the hardest to measure, and
teaches staff very little about using AI in their own work.

A good first pilot is the opposite. Internal, repetitive, measurable, and
owned by someone who wants it to work.

## Four tests for a first use case

Score each candidate against these. A good first pilot passes all four.

1. **No restricted data.** The work can be done without customer or
   member information. That removes most of the risk and most of the
   approval friction.
2. **Repeated often.** The task happens weekly or daily, so a small time
   saving compounds and staff get enough practice to improve.
3. **Easy to check.** A person can tell quickly whether the output is
   right. Drafts and summaries qualify; predictions do not.
4. **A willing owner.** A department head who has the problem, wants it
   solved, and will report honestly on whether it worked.

Candidates that usually pass: rewriting procedures into plain language,
summarizing committee meetings with no customer details, comparing an
old and new version of a policy, first drafts of routine internal
reports, and building training scenarios with invented customers. The
[role playbooks](/playbooks) list more by department.

## Write a one-page charter

Before anyone opens a tool, agree on:

- **The task**, in one sentence, and who owns it
- **The tool**, approved by IT under your vendor process
- **The data rule** — what may and may not go in
- **Review** — who checks the output and what they look for
- **The measure** — time per task before and after, plus a quality check
- **The end date** and who decides what happens next

Record it in your AI use case inventory as a pilot. Our
[AI workflow SOP template](/resources/templates/ai-workflow-sop) gives you
the structure.

## Measure honestly

Time the task for a week before the pilot so you have a real baseline.
During the pilot, record time per task and how much the reviewer had to
change. At the end you want three answers: did it save time, was the
quality acceptable, and did people keep using it once the novelty wore
off. The third one matters most.

Report what you find, including what did not work. A pilot that fails
cleanly and cheaply is a good outcome; it tells you where not to spend.

## After the pilot

If it worked, write the procedure, train the rest of the team, and move
it from pilot to approved use in the inventory. Then pick the next use
case — this time perhaps one that touches restricted data or reaches
customers, now that your people have practiced the basics.

If you are not sure which workflows in your institution are ready, that
is exactly what readiness assessments are for.
