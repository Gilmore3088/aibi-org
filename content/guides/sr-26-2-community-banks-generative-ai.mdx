In April 2026 the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC replaced the model risk
guidance that had shaped bank practice for fifteen years. SR 26-2
superseded SR 11-7, the original 2011 guidance, and SR 21-8, the 2021
statement on BSA/AML models. For community bankers, two questions follow
immediately: is this aimed at us, and does it cover the AI tools our
people are actually using?

## Who SR 26-2 is aimed at

The Federal Reserve's letter is direct about it: SR 26-2 is expected to
be most relevant to banking organizations with over $30 billion in total
assets that the Fed regulates. It also says model risk management
practices should vary with each institution's risk profile and how it
uses models.

That is not an exemption. A community bank that uses models for credit
scoring, pricing, ALLL or CECL estimates, interest rate risk, or BSA/AML
monitoring still needs to understand them, know their limits, and keep
evidence that someone checked. What SR 26-2 signals is proportion: your
program should fit the number and importance of your models, not mirror a
regional bank's.

## Why generative AI sits outside its formal scope

SR 26-2 is non-binding, and generative and agentic AI are outside its
formal scope while the agencies gather input. So the guidance most people
expected to answer "how do we govern ChatGPT-style tools" deliberately
does not.

Do not read that as a gap you can wait out. GAO's 2025 review found that
regulators oversee AI through existing laws, guidance, and examinations.
Generative AI used at your institution still runs into your information
security program, privacy obligations, third-party risk management, fair
lending and UDAAP expectations, and records requirements. And state
examiners now have the CSBS AI Supervisory Framework, which treats
generative AI as its own review area.

## What to do with this

**Sort your AI into two lists.** Your inventory should separate
traditional models — the ones SR 26-2 contemplates — from generative AI
tools used for drafting, summarizing, and search. The first list gets
model risk treatment scaled to your size. The second gets governance
through your AI policy, data rules, vendor diligence, and human review.

**Right-size the model list.** For each traditional model: an owner, a
description of what it does and its known limits, the last time someone
checked its performance, and what you would do if it failed. For vendor
models, the vendor's validation evidence plus your own outcomes
monitoring.

**Give generative AI its own page.** A short section of your AI policy, or
a standalone procedure, covering approved tools, the data line, and who
reviews output. That page is what an examiner looking at generative AI
will ask for — and SR 26-2 will not have written it for you.

**Retire the SR 11-7 references.** Policies and procedures that still cite
SR 11-7 as current guidance should be updated to reflect that SR 26-2
superseded it. It is a small fix that signals your program is current.
