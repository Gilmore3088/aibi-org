At many community banks and credit unions, AI does not arrive as a project. It arrives in a vendor's release notes, inside a renewal, or
attached to a demo that looks very good. Which makes vendor due diligence
the place where most of your AI risk is actually decided.

You do not need a new process. The Interagency TPRM Guidance from the
Federal Reserve, FDIC, and OCC (June 2023) organizes third-party risk
management around a five-stage life cycle — planning, due diligence and
third-party selection, contract negotiation, ongoing monitoring, and
termination — and expects oversight to be commensurate with your size,
complexity, and the risk of the relationship.

In September 2026 those agencies and NCUA proposed guidance that would
rescind and replace it, with comments due November 16, 2026. The proposal
keeps due diligence, contract negotiation, ongoing monitoring, and
termination, and puts more weight on tailoring oversight to each
relationship's risk and on deciding when residual risk is acceptable. The
questions below work under either version. AI vendors fit that structure.
What they need is better questions.

## Planning: decide what you are buying

Before the demo, write down in one paragraph what the AI will do, what
data it will touch, and whether it informs a person or acts on its own.
That paragraph sets the risk tier and becomes the entry in your
[AI use case inventory](/guides/ai-use-case-inventory-bank). If no one can
write it, you are not ready to evaluate a vendor.

## Due diligence: the core questions

Send these in writing and keep the answers.

**What the AI does**

- Which features in the product use AI or machine learning, and what does
  each one do?
- Is the AI built by you, or does it rely on a third-party model provider?
  Which one?
- Does the AI make or recommend decisions about customers or members?

**Your data**

- What of our data does the AI process, and where is it processed and
  stored?
- Is our data, or data derived from it, used to train or improve your
  models or anyone else's? Can we opt out in the contract?
- How long are inputs and outputs retained, and how are they deleted?
- Who at your company, or at your model provider, can see our data?

**Accuracy, testing, and change**

- How do you test the AI's accuracy and reliability, and how often?
- What known limitations or failure modes should our staff know about?
- How will you notify us before changing the AI or its underlying model?
- If the AI is used in credit decisions, how do you support the specific
  reasons required for adverse action notices and fair lending testing?

**Oversight and incidents**

- Can we see output logs and audit trails for our use?
- Can a person review or override every AI output before it takes effect?
- How do you notify us of an incident involving the AI or our data?
- What independent assessments — SOC reports, penetration tests, model
  validations — can you share?

## Contract negotiation: the terms that matter most

The 2023 guidance recognizes that a banking organization may have limited
negotiating power, and the September 2026 proposal goes further, discussing
when residual risk can be accepted if terms cannot be negotiated. Spend the
leverage you have where it counts:

1. **No training on your data** without your written consent.
2. **Advance notice of material changes** to AI functionality or the
   underlying model.
3. **Retention and deletion** terms for inputs, outputs, and logs.
4. **Audit and testing rights**, or at least the right to receive the
   vendor's own testing results.
5. **Incident notification** that explicitly covers AI-related failures.

## Ongoing monitoring

Assign a business owner who reads release notes and tracks complaints and
errors tied to the AI. A reasonable default is to review high-risk AI vendors at least annually, and
whenever the vendor changes the model or adds a feature. Each change gets
a line in the inventory.

## Termination

Know before you sign how you get your data back, how the vendor deletes
it — including from any model provider it used — and how you would run
the process without the AI if you had to.

## A note for credit unions

The 2023 interagency guidance applies to banks; credit unions follow
NCUA's third-party expectations, though NCUA joined the 2026 proposal. The questions above work under either.
One difference raises the stakes: GAO reported in 2025 that NCUA lacks
the authority to examine technology service providers. Because NCUA cannot examine the vendor directly, your own diligence file
carries more of the weight.
