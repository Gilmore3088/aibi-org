ECOA and Regulation B were written long before machine learning reached
loan origination systems, and they do not need updating to apply to it.
The rule is technology-neutral: when you take adverse action, the
applicant is entitled to the specific principal reasons. If your
institution cannot say why the system said no, it has a compliance
problem, whatever the system is.

## What the regulation actually requires

Regulation B, at 12 CFR 1002.9(b)(2), requires that the statement of
reasons be specific and indicate the principal reason or reasons for the
adverse action. It also closes the obvious shortcut: statements that the
action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies, or
that the applicant failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's
scoring system, are insufficient.

Read that against an AI-assisted decision. "Our model scored you below
the cutoff" is precisely the kind of reason the regulation says is not
enough. The notice has to name what drove the result.

## Where AI creates the problem

Many community lenders do not build credit models themselves. The exposure
usually comes from three places:

- **Vendor decisioning tools** that produce an approve, refer, or decline
  without a clear list of contributing factors.
- **Scoring add-ons** that combine many variables in ways the vendor
  describes only at a high level.
- **Informal AI use** — an underwriter asking a general AI tool to
  summarize a file or suggest a decision, where the tool's influence on
  the outcome is undocumented.

The first two are diligence questions. The third is a policy question.

## What to check now

1. **Inventory every AI touchpoint in lending.** Application intake,
   document capture, decisioning, pricing, and notice generation. Each goes into your [AI use case inventory](/guides/ai-use-case-inventory-bank);
   anything that influences the decision, pricing, or the notice is high
   risk.
2. **Ask each vendor for a reason-code walkthrough.** Pick a real declined
   application and have the vendor show how its tool produces the
   principal reasons that end up on your notice. If it cannot, that is
   your answer.
3. **Test the notices.** Have compliance pull a sample of recent
   AI-assisted declines and confirm the stated reasons match the factors
   that actually drove each decision. The official commentary to
   Regulation B says the reasons must relate to and accurately describe the
   factors actually considered or scored.
4. **Draw the line on informal use.** Your AI policy should say plainly
   that general-purpose AI tools may not make or recommend credit
   decisions, and that restricted applicant data never goes into an
   unapproved tool.
5. **Keep a person accountable.** Every adverse action should trace to a
   human decision-maker or an approved, documented process — never to an
   unreviewed AI output.

## Where AI can help safely

There is useful, low-risk work here. AI can help compliance rewrite
notice templates in plainer language, build training scenarios with
invented applicants, and summarize long regulatory text for review. The
[lending playbook](/playbooks/lending) has examples. In every case the
output is a draft a person checks, and the reasons themselves come from
the decision record, never from the AI.

## Fair lending is the larger frame

Adverse action is the most visible obligation, not the only one. Any
model or AI tool that touches credit should be part of your fair lending
monitoring, with outcomes reviewed across protected classes. Ask vendors
what fair lending testing they perform and what results they can share,
and record their answers in your diligence file.
