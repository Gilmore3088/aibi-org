An AI acceptable use policy has one job: tell every employee what they may
do with AI, what they may not, and who to ask when they are unsure. It is
not a strategy document and it is not a model risk framework. Institutions
that try to make it either one end up with a draft that never leaves
committee — while staff keep using whatever is on their phone.

There is no AI-specific banking regulation that dictates what the policy
must contain. GAO-25-107197 found that oversight of AI in financial
services works through existing frameworks rather than a dedicated AI
rule. That is freeing. It means your policy can be short, written in your
own terms, and tied to obligations you already manage: information
security, third-party risk, fair lending, and records.

## What the policy must cover

Seven sections carry almost all of the weight. If your draft has these,
it is a working policy.

1. **Purpose and scope.** Who it applies to (employees, contractors,
   directors) and what counts as AI. Keep the definition broad: any tool
   that generates text, images, code, summaries, or recommendations,
   including AI features built into software you already license.
2. **Approved tools.** Name them, or point to a list management maintains.
   Separate tools approved for customer or member information from tools
   approved only for public or internal, non-sensitive work.
3. **The data line.** The single most important sentence in the policy.
   State in plain words which information may never be entered into an
   unapproved tool: customer and member nonpublic personal information,
   account numbers, credit files, examination findings, and confidential
   strategy. Give examples staff will recognize.
4. **Human review.** AI output is a draft. A named person reviews it before
   it reaches a customer or member, a regulator, or the file. Say which
   uses need a second reviewer — anything touching a credit decision, a
   disclosure, or a complaint response.
5. **Prohibited uses.** Short and specific. For example: no AI-only credit
   decisions, no AI-generated content presented to customers as if a
   person wrote it where that would mislead, no uploading of vendor
   contracts or exam reports to public tools.
6. **New uses and new tools.** How someone proposes a new AI use or tool,
   who reviews it, and that approved uses get recorded in the AI use case
   inventory.
7. **Ownership, training, and exceptions.** Who owns the policy, how staff
   are trained on it, how exceptions are requested, and what happens when
   someone breaks it.

## What to leave out

Leave the tool list, prompt guidance, and step-by-step procedures out of
the policy itself. Those change monthly. Put them in a procedure or a
staff reference card that management can update without returning to the
board. The policy points to them; it does not contain them.

Leave out aspirational language about transformation and innovation. An
examiner reading the policy is looking for the rules and the owner, not
the vision.

## Write it so a teller can follow it

The test for every sentence: could a teller, a loan assistant, or a new
marketing hire read it and know what to do on Monday? Phrases like
"appropriate safeguards" and "where reasonable" fail that test. "Never
paste a customer name, account number, or Social Security number into a
tool that is not on the approved list" passes.

A one-page staff summary of the data line and the approved tools does
more for actual behavior than the policy itself. Write both.

## Getting it approved

Most institutions stall in review, not drafting. Three moves keep it
moving:

- **Draft from a starter, not a blank page.** Our
  [AI use policy starter](/resources/templates/ai-use-policy-starter) has
  the seven sections above, written for a community institution.
- **Route it like your other risk policies.** Management drafts,
  your risk or technology committee reviews, the board approves. Do not
  invent a new governance path for this one document.
- **Approve the policy and the first tool list together.** A policy that
  approves nothing pushes staff straight back to unapproved tools.

## What examiners are likely to look for

The CSBS Artificial Intelligence Supervisory Framework, released in
September 2026, gives state examiners a document request list and review
procedures covering governance, the AI inventory, and generative AI. It is
discretionary and creates no new requirements. But it is a clear signal
that "do you have a policy, who owns it, and how do you know it is
followed" will be early questions. Read our
[briefing on the CSBS framework](/briefings/csbs-ai-exam-framework) for
what to pre-stage.

The non-binding FS AI RMF from Treasury and the financial sector gives
larger programs more structure if you want it. A community bank does not
need to adopt it to have a sound policy.

## A realistic timeline

A small team can produce a reviewed draft in two to three weeks: one week
to draft from a starter and agree on the data line, one week for
compliance, IT, and legal comments, and the next committee cycle for
approval. If yours has been in review for a quarter, the problem is scope.
Cut it back to the seven sections and move the rest to procedures.
