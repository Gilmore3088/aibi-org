Most board AI briefings fail in one of two directions. Either they are a
vendor-style tour of what AI can do, which leaves directors impressed and
no better equipped to oversee it. Or they are a list of risks with no
sense of proportion, which leaves directors anxious and inclined to say
no to everything. Neither helps the board do its job.

The board's job here is the same as everywhere else: set direction,
approve the policy, and hold management accountable. A good briefing
gives directors exactly what they need for those three things.

## The first briefing: one page, five sections

**1. Where we use AI today.** The honest list, from your AI use case
inventory — including AI embedded in vendor products. Three columns:
what it does, who owns it, and its risk tier.

**2. How it is governed.** The policy that covers it (or the plan to
adopt one), who approves new uses, the rule on customer and member data,
and how output is reviewed before it reaches anyone outside the building.

**3. The risks that matter for us.** Not a generic list. The three or four
that apply to your actual uses: data leaving the institution through
unapproved tools, vendor AI changing without notice, inaccurate output
reaching a customer, and any AI touching credit decisions.

**4. The opportunity, in plain numbers we can stand behind.** Where AI is
saving staff time or could, described as specific workflows. Avoid
projections you cannot source; directors remember them.

**5. What we are asking the board for.** Approval of the policy, comment
on the risk appetite, or simply acknowledgment. Be explicit.

## What to leave out

Industry hype. Demos. Technical architecture. Peer comparisons you cannot
source. And anything that implies the board should approve individual
tools — that is management's job, under a policy the board approves.

## The quarterly update

After the first briefing, keep it to half a page:

- Changes to the AI inventory since last quarter
- New tools or uses approved, and anything declined
- Incidents, complaints, or near misses involving AI
- Policy exceptions granted
- Training completed
- Anything regulators said or asked about AI

If nothing changed, say so in one line. Consistency matters more than
length; a governance trail is built from short, regular entries.

## Why examiners will care

The CSBS AI Supervisory Framework gives state examiners procedures for
governance and oversight of AI. Board minutes that show directors were
briefed, asked questions, and approved the policy are part of the
evidence of that oversight. Our
[board briefing checklist](/resources/templates/board-briefing-checklist)
turns the structure above into a reusable template.

## Questions directors should be asking

Share these with your board so they know what good oversight sounds like:

- What AI do we use, and how do we know the list is complete?
- What customer or member information does it touch?
- Who reviews AI output before it reaches a customer or member?
- Which vendors provide our AI, and what did we learn in diligence?
- How do we know employees follow the policy?
- What would we do if an AI tool failed or produced something harmful?

A management team that can answer these crisply is in good shape. One
that cannot has its next quarter's work plan.
